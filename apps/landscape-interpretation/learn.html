Processes that write a place

Every hillside is a collaboration — ice, water, fire, plants, animals, and people. Use plain clues. Prefer “suggests” over “proves.”

Field reader · Volunteer nearby · Fieldry

Glaciation

Ice sheets dragged and dropped mixed rock. Erratics, till, kettles, and poorly sorted soils often remain. Ask: is this rock local and angular, or far-travelled and rounded?

Historic agriculture

Stone walls, fence wire in trees, and even-aged wall lines often mark old fields. Forest that “grew back” after pasture abandonment can look dense and young inside an old enclosure.

Logging

Stumps, skid trails, and sudden even-aged cohorts point to cutting. The forest may be healthy — and still tell a harvest story.

Fire

Basal scars, charcoal, and patchy deadwood can show fire’s hand. Confirm patterns across a slope before assuming a landscape-scale burn.

Flooding & hydrology

Wrack lines, scour, terraces, and plants that tolerate wet feet record high water. A stream valley that floods repeatedly looks different from an upland seep.

Mining

Spoil, pits, and odd metal debris can reshape soils for centuries. Stay out of workings; treat readings as educational, not contamination surveys.

Succession

Meadows become shrubs, then trees. Dense young stems beside open forbs often mean an opening is closing — from farm, beaver, blowdown, or cut.

Soils & topography

Thin soils over bedrock, soft seepage ground, and terrace steps change what can grow. Form and drainage teach as much as plant lists.

Infrastructure

Roadcuts, utility corridors, and graded berms create sharp edges and rewritten drainage — human geometry laid over older landforms.

How to observe

  1. Name what you see before naming a story.
  2. Collect two or three independent clues.
  3. List alternatives that still fit.
  4. Walk one more transect before raising confidence.
