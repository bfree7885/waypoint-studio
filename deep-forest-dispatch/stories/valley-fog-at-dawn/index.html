Why do valleys fill with fog at dawn?

From a ridge at first light, the valley below can look like a white lake — shorelines of fog following every creek and hollow. The air did not flood in from elsewhere. Overnight, the landscape drained cold air into its own low places until the valley saturated.

A white lake that was never water

Stand above a valley just after dawn and the floor may vanish under a continuous white sheet. Ridges and upper slopes often remain clear. Roads drop into a wall of cloud. From a satellite, the same event can look like white veins tracing every drainage.

That pattern is a clue. The fog is not randomly scattered weather. It is collecting where cold air collects.

Overnight: the ground loses heat

Under clear skies and light winds, the ground radiates heat to space after sunset. Air in contact with that cooling surface cools as well. Meteorologists call this radiational cooling.

Calm conditions matter. Stronger winds mix warmer air from above and keep the surface layer from chilling as efficiently. Cloud cover acts like a lid, reducing the overnight heat loss that seeds many fog nights.

National Weather Service explainers treat classic radiation fog as a clear-night, light-wind process — the starting point for many valley events.

Cold air drains downhill

Cooler air is denser. On slopes, that density difference becomes motion: chilled air slides downhill as cold-air drainage (a gentle katabatic tendency under quiet weather).

Valleys are traps. Night after night of drainage fills the basin from the bottom up with a pool of cold air, while ridges may stay comparatively milder and clearer. A shallow temperature inversion often forms — cold air below, warmer air above.

When temperature meets the dew point

Fog is cloud with its base on the ground. It forms when the air becomes saturated — when temperature approaches the dew point closely enough for water vapor to condense on tiny particles.

In a draining valley, cooling does much of the work. Moisture evaporating from damp ground, rivers, or lakes can raise the dew point and help. That is why fog often hugs stream corridors first and then deepens through the night.

Many valley fogs reach peak density near sunrise, when overnight cooling has run longest. Higher slopes may never reach saturation at all — so they stay visually clear while the basin looks flooded.

Day → night → fog → burn-off

This sequence is the Waypoint field-guide version of the NWS story: cooling, drainage, pooling, saturation, then morning mixing.

Why the fog often leaves after sunrise

Sunlight warms the ground. The surface layer heats, mixes, and relative humidity falls. Fog thins, lifts, or breaks into patches — the familiar “burn-off,” which can be quick on open flats and slower in shaded mountain valleys.

Not every event ends that way. Deep winter cold pools and persistent inversions can hold fog or low stratus for hours or days. The dawn valley fill is the common, photogenic case — not the only one.

Not every fog is this fog

Weather services distinguish fog types carefully. Advection fog forms when moist air moves over a colder surface. Upslope fog forms when air is forced up terrain and cools. Steam fog can rise when cold air overlies warmer water.

Valley radiation fog is the story when overnight cooling plus drainage dominates. Real mornings can blend mechanisms. The useful habit is to ask what cooled, what moved, and where moisture came from — not to force every white valley into one label.
